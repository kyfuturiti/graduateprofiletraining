    Welcome, there. Let’s start with why.

    The Kentucky Graduate Profile names 10 Essential Skills that every graduate of a Kentucky public college or university should be able to show. This training covers what those skills are and how to build them into the courses you already teach.

    💡

    What these numbers mean for your classroom

    Students feel ready. Employers aren’t so sure, and more of them now ask candidates to show a skill instead of pointing to a degree. Your students already build many of these skills in your course. What they often lack is the words to name a skill and the practice to show it. The Graduate Profile gives you and your students those words.

    From what students know to what they can do

    The Graduate Profile asks you to plan your course around two questions instead of one.

    The familiar question

    “What do my students need to know?”

    The theories, facts, methods, and vocabulary of your discipline.

    The Graduate Profile question

    “What do my students need to be able to do?”

    Explain an idea to a real audience, weigh evidence, solve a problem with a team, and use what they learned on the job.

    The second question doesn’t replace the first. Your discipline’s content is how students build these skills, and the skills are how that content shows up after graduation.

    💡

    “Isn’t this just a compliance exercise?”

    It isn’t meant to be. CPE developed the Graduate Profile with faculty, staff, and employers so students can see the purpose of general education, how it connects to their major, and how their major connects to life after college. It also helps answer a question students and families ask all the time: is college worth it?

    The framework doesn’t add a new subject to your course. It names the value of what you already teach.

    Section 2 of 11

    Ten skills, three levels. How does it fit together?

    The Kentucky Graduate Profile is the state’s postsecondary learning framework. It connects the knowledge, skills, and abilities students gain in college with the skills they need at work and in life.

    📜

    From the Kentucky Graduate Profile

    “All students graduating from public postsecondary institutions in Kentucky should have multiple, intentional opportunities to develop these 10 Essential Skills through the institution’s curriculum and co-curricular offerings.”

    Kentucky Council on Postsecondary Education

    Notice the words multiple and intentional. A student won’t master teamwork or information literacy in a single general education course. These skills grow when students practice them again and again, on purpose, across their whole program. Your courses are part of that.

    1. 2021

    2. Development

    4. Today

      Campus teams and new faculty

      Each institution has a Graduate Profile leadership team of academic leaders, faculty, and student affairs staff. This training is part of a toolkit that helps new faculty join that work.

    The 10 Essential Skills

    You’ll look closely at each one in Section 3. For now, the full set:

      Three levels of mastery

      For every skill, CPE describes performance at three levels. Together they show how a student grows from finishing high school to finishing a bachelor’s degree.

      1. Level 1

        Benchmark

        Where students should be when they start college

        CPE definition

        Learners are beginning to acquire the knowledge or skill but may need guidance or support. Benchmark is how a learner would perform at the level of high school completion.

      2. Level 2

        Milestone

        Where students should be about two years in

        CPE definition

        Learners can demonstrate competence independently. This is the performance expected at the associate’s level or halfway through a bachelor’s degree program.

      3. Level 3

        Capstone

        Where students should be when they earn a bachelor’s degree

        CPE definition

        Learners can do at the completion of their bachelor’s degree, applying the competency with the expertise and precision.

      What growth looks like

      Take Communication as one example. A Benchmark student asks thoughtful clarifying questions. By Capstone, that student isn’t just asking questions. They’re facilitating professional conversations.

      Quick check

      Where does each student fit?

      A first-semester student writes a solid first paper after you share a model paper and a step-by-step checklist.

      A student halfway through a bachelor’s program designs a short survey for a class project, runs it without step-by-step directions, and explains what the results show.

      A graduating senior leads a meeting with a community partner for a capstone project, adjusts the plan when the partner’s priorities change, and presents clear, well-supported recommendations.

      Where your students are coming from

      Kentucky’s K-12 learning framework is the Portrait of a Learner, adopted by the Kentucky Department of Education. CPE and KDE worked together so the two frameworks line up. Pick a Graduate Profile skill to see the Portrait of a Learner traits that match it.

      Why the K-12 connection helps

      A student who heard “Productive Collaborator” in high school already has a starting point for Collaboration and Teamwork in your course. Saying that out loud helps students see these skills as something they’ve been building for years, not a new set of rules.

        Section 4 of 11

        Five to ten minutes. No new unit required.

        Small Teaching comes from James M. Lang. You make minor, deliberate tweaks to what you already do so an Essential Skill gets intentional space in your course.

        A small teaching move can take as little as five to ten minutes of class time. It builds on your expertise and the course you already have, so you’re revising, not rebuilding. It works well for small but meaningful changes to assignments you already give.

        💡

        “I don’t have time to add more content.”

        You don’t need to. Small teaching doesn’t add a topic. It changes how students work with the content you’re already covering, often in the last few minutes of class.

        Example: the Minute Thesis

        Builds

        Critical and Creative Thinking Information Literacy
        The strategy
        At the very end of a lecture or reading discussion, give students 60 seconds to write down the central argument or a key takeaway of the session, along with one piece of evidence supporting it.
        In practice
        In a Sociology course discussing community structures, the instructor saves the last two minutes of class. Students write down one major takeaway about urban development and list one source of data from their readings that proves it.
        Why it works
        Students have to pull the idea from memory right away, which strengthens what they learned. Naming the evidence builds information literacy on the spot.

        Three moments in any class session

        Small teaching leans on quick moves like predicting and retrieving. Each one fits into a class you already teach.

        1. Start of class

          Predict

          Before a demonstration, case, or data set, ask students to predict what will happen and why.

          Builds Critical and Creative Thinking

        2. Middle of class

          Connect

          Pause and ask students where today’s concept would show up in a job or situation they know.

          Builds Knowledge Application

        3. Last two minutes

          Retrieve

          The Minute Thesis: one takeaway from today and one piece of evidence that supports it.

          Builds Information Literacy

        Quick check

        Which of these is a small teaching move?

        Say when it happens (start, middle, or end of class), what students will do, and which skill it builds.

        Section 5 of 11

        Purpose, task, criteria. Say it before students start.

        Transparency in Learning and Teaching, or TILT, comes from Mary-Ann Winkelmes. It makes learning processes explicit to students.

        Clearly communicating the why and how of an assignment before students begin can dramatically improve student performance, particularly among first-generation and underrepresented students. For the Graduate Profile, that means stating the Essential Skill at the outset and reinforcing it throughout the assignment and the course.

        Every major assignment answers three things

        • Purpose

          The exact skills and knowledge students will gain, and how they connect to a future career.

        • Task

          Step-by-step instructions for what students need to do.

        • Criteria

          A transparent rubric or checklist showing what a successful product looks like before students turn it in.

        Before and after

        Instead of assigning a vague research paper, a business instructor TILTs a project management assignment.

        Before

        BUS 210 Project Management

        Research Paper

        Write a 10-page research paper on a topic related to the course. Use at least five sources.

        Due Week 12.

        Which skill is this for? What does a good one look like?

        After TILT

        BUS 210 Project Management

        Vendor Risk Memo

        CommunicationProfessionalism

        Purpose

        To build professional executive communication skills.

        Task

        1. Conduct a risk analysis of a local vendor.
        2. Write a three-page memo.

        Criteria for success

        An annotated example of an “A” paper, highlighting the professional formatting and clear tone required.

        Try it

        Sort each line from an assignment sheet into Purpose, Task, or Criteria.

        “This assignment builds the written communication you’ll use when you advise a manager or a client.”

        “Interview two people who work in the field and take notes on their answers.”

        “A strong memo opens with a clear recommendation and backs it up with at least two pieces of evidence.”

        “Submit a one-page outline by Friday, then your full draft the following week.”

        “An annotated A-level example is posted so you can see the tone and formatting expected.”

        “You’ll practice judging sources, which you’ll need for making decisions in any career.”

        💡

        Name the skill, and students can name it too

        Back in the second poll, most graduating seniors didn’t know the term employers use for hiring on skills. When the Purpose names an Essential Skill, students get the words to describe what they can do on a resume or in an interview.

        Section 6 of 11

        Start with the end. Then work backward.

        Backward Design comes from Grant Wiggins and Jay McTighe. Rather than starting course planning with content (“What textbook chapters should I cover?”), you start with the end in mind.

        It’s ideal for designing a new course or redesigning one. It gives you a process for making sure a course teaches, and supports learning of, the 10 Essential Skills.

        Three stages, in order

        Example: an Environmental Science course where the instructor wants students to master Collaboration and Teamwork

        1. Stage 1

          Identify desired results

          What should students be able to do by the end?

          Students will work cohesively to manage conflict and solve a complex ecological problem.

        2. Stage 2

          Determine the evidence

          How will you know students can do it?

          A group-led environmental impact presentation and an accompanying team-dynamics reflection rubric.

        3. Stage 3

          Plan learning experiences

          What will students practice along the way?

          Low-stakes weekly group labs where roles rotate, like Data Collector and Project Lead, so students practice team skills before the final project.

        Section 7 of 11

        Put the skills in writing. Start with your syllabus.

        Your syllabus tells students what the course is for. When your objectives name an Essential Skill, students know what they’re practicing, and the objective connects to the assignments and assessments that build it.

        The goal is to embed the skills in the course you already have. Aim for at least two or three Essential Skills explicitly stated in your course learning objectives.

          Section 8 of 11

          Grade the content. Grade the skill, too.

          The goal is to measure the Essential Skills fairly and accurately, alongside general education and the content of your discipline.

          💡

          “You can’t really grade skills like teamwork or critical thinking.”

          You can, and CPE has already done much of the groundwork. Teams of faculty, staff, and employers wrote measurable indicators for every skill at each of the three levels. The indicators describe what a student does, so you can observe it and score it.

          Try it: score Jordan’s presentation

          J

          Jordan

          Junior, EGR 310 Structural Analysis

          Jordan presented a bridge design to a panel that included a local engineer. His calculations were correct, and he earned full marks on Part A. Now score his communication, one row at a time.

          Listening and conversation

          When the engineer asked a question, Jordan gave her his full attention, asked a follow-up to make sure he understood, and noticed when she looked unconvinced. He answered each question but didn’t open the discussion to the rest of the panel.

          Writing

          Jordan’s handout opened with a one-page summary written for the non-engineers on the panel, followed by a detailed section with technical vocabulary and a logical structure for the engineer.

          Speaking and presenting

          Jordan read most of his slides word for word. He covered all the information and responded to the panel’s ideas with ideas of his own, but his talk had no clear opening or conclusion.

          💡

          Strong content didn’t guarantee strong communication

          Jordan earned full marks on content and still has room to grow as a communicator. A rubric with both parts shows him where, and it gives your program evidence of the skill.
